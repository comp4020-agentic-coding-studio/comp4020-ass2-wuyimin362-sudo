Ten weeks of this course have been about your position. This week is about the
sentence "your position", which is doing more work than it looks.

You do not, in most arrangements, hold the thing you bought. A broker holds it,
or a custodian holds it for the broker, or the thing itself is a promise from an
issuer who owes you a payment later. In each case what you own is an **entry on
somebody else's books** saying you are owed something. That entry is excellent
when the somebody is solvent and worthless in the specific case where they are
not.

**Counterparty risk** is the chance that the other side of your arrangement fails
to do their part. It has no relationship to whether you were right about the
instrument. You can pick correctly, size sensibly, pay little in fees, hold
patiently, and still finish with nothing, because the failure happened in the
plumbing rather than in the market.

This is the only week on the syllabus that nobody in the room will experience.
It is rare, it cannot be committed on purpose, it cannot happen on a paper
account, and a $20 position is too small to be worth anyone's fraud. It is
taught because knowing that the category exists changes one thing you do: you
read where your assets are held before you need to know.

So that is this week's work, and it involves no trading. Find out, in writing,
who holds what you own — the broker, the custodian behind them, whether client
assets are held separately from the firm's own, and what happens to your entry
if the firm stops operating. Bring the document, not a summary of it. Most
people in the room will be reading it for the first time, eleven weeks after
opening a position on the strength of it.
