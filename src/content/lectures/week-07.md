Open your [opening record](/records/) and read question 2 — the one sentence
beginning "I am buying it because".

A position pays you in exactly one of two ways. The thing you own hands you
cash — rent, a dividend, interest, a share of profits — which is called a **cash
flow** and arrives whether or not anyone wants to buy from you. Or somebody pays
you more than you paid. That second one requires a buyer, and the buyer's
willingness is a fact about them rather than about the thing.

Most sentences written in week 1 describe neither. They describe a narrative:
what the thing is going to become, who has started paying attention to it, what
it is obviously about to do. A narrative is not nothing — it is a real reason
prices move, because it is what the next buyer is buying too. It is a reason
that can stop without anything happening to the underlying thing at all.

## Turn the price into a claim

Here is the arithmetic that makes this checkable rather than a matter of taste.

A price is a number somebody paid for a claim on future cash. So you can run it
backwards: given the price, **what would this thing have to earn for the price
to make sense?**

Suppose comparable things change hands at around fifteen times what they earn in
a year. Your position is 610 units of B at $18.00. Then

```
implied earnings  =  $18.00 / 15
                  =  $1.20 per unit per year
```

and across the position

```
610 × $1.20  =  $732 per year
```

That is the claim you bought. Not a prediction — an implication of the price you
actually paid. Somebody, somewhere, has to believe this thing produces about
$732 a year for your 610 units, or that the next buyer will believe it.

Now the question that matters: **do you know what it actually earns?**

For most of the room the answer is no, and it was no in week 1 as well. That is
the finding. You are not being told the position is bad; you are being shown
that you have no way to tell, because you never held a number that could be
compared with $1.20.

## When there is no cash at all

Some things pay nothing and are not expected to. Then the arithmetic above has
no answer, and the price rests entirely on resale.

Check the other direction. If a thing priced at $18.00 paid you $0.50 a year,

```
$18.00 / $0.50  =  36 years
```

before the payments alone return what you put in. At zero a year the answer is
not a large number — there is no number. Every dollar you get back has to come
from a person, and that person needs a reason, and the reason is the narrative
you are holding.

This is not an argument against owning such a thing. It is an argument for
knowing which of the two you own, because they fail differently and you cannot
tell them apart by looking at the price.

## A story pausing and a story ending

Here is the part that makes this a failure mode rather than an observation.

If you hold a cash flow, you have something that generates a number on its own
schedule, so you can tell a bad quarter from a broken business. If you hold a
narrative, you have no such instrument. The price falls, and you cannot
distinguish "attention has moved elsewhere for a while" from "this is over",
because the only evidence either way is the price itself.

So the position becomes unfalsifiable, and an unfalsifiable position is held
until something else forces the decision. Week 4 named how you got in. This week
names why you felt entitled to, and week 10 will show you what it costs to stay.

## This week

Two things, both short.

Compute your own implied earnings: your price, a multiple you can justify, and
the per-year figure that falls out. Put it in your [log](/records/) whether or
not you can check it against anything, and write "unknown" where it is unknown.

Then bring your week 1 sentence to the session unedited. We are going to sort
the room's sentences into cash flows and narratives, out loud, and the sort is
usually lopsided in a way people find uncomfortable to watch.
