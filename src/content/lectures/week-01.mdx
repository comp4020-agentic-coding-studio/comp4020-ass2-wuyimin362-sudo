Here is a bet. Nineteen times in twenty it pays you 10%. One time in twenty it
takes everything you staked.

Work out what it is worth on average. The multiplier per bet is

```
(19/20) × 1.10  +  (1/20) × 0
  =  0.95 × 1.10
  =  1.045
```

so the bet pays 4.5% per go. That is a good bet. Take it forty times and the
average outcome is

```
1.045^40  =  5.82
```

nearly six times your money. Every part of that is correct and you should not
take the bet.

## Why not

To finish forty bets you have to survive all forty, and the chance of that is

```
(19/20)^40  =  0.95^40  =  0.1285
```

Thirteen people in a hundred. The other eighty-seven are at zero, and zero is
not a bad result you recover from next semester — it is the end of the sequence,
because nothing multiplies back up out of nothing.

Both numbers are true at once, and reconciling them is the whole of this
lecture. A hundred people take the bet forty times. Between them they end with
582 units of whatever they started with, which is the 5.82 average. But only
12.85 of them are holding any of it, so the survivors are sitting on

```
1.10^40  =  45.26
```

about forty-five times their stake each. Note that this is not an average over a
range of good outcomes: a survivor is someone the loss never happened to, so
every survivor multiplied their stake by 1.10 exactly forty times and they all
hold the identical amount. There is no spread among the winners to explain.

Those thirteen will be asked how they did it. They will have answers. The
answers will be sincere and they will be worthless, because the eighty-seven did
the same thing.

This is called gambler's ruin. The name is old and the arithmetic above is all
of it.

## What the course does with this

The claim is not that you should never take a bet with a chance of total loss.
It is narrower and harder to escape: **if you take such a bet repeatedly, the
expected value of the bet stops being the thing that decides your outcome.** The
number that decides it is how many times you are exposed, and that number is
usually a choice you made without noticing you were making it.

Drag the first slider above to see the shape of that. At one bet you survive 95%
of the time. At forty you are down to 12.85%. At a hundred it is 0.59%, and
nothing about the bet changed — only how often you accepted it.

Watch the bottom two rows while you drag, because they move in opposite
directions. At forty bets the average outcome is 5.82 times your stake. At a
hundred bets it is

```
1.045^100  =  81.59
```

**The bet has become fourteen times more attractive by the only measure most
people use, and your chance of being there to collect has fallen from 12.85% to
0.59%.** Both of those are consequences of the same act: accepting it more
often. There is no version of this where the average is the thing to look at.

You will spend eleven weeks finding out that the chance of total loss is not
exotic. It is in the fees, in the position size, in the borrowed money, in the
door that turns out to be locked. Each of the next ten weeks names one of them
and shows you the arithmetic.

## You do not believe this yet

That is expected, and it is planned for. Today the argument is abstract: it is
about a bet nobody offered you, in a sequence you have not run. There is no
reason it should land.

So in this session you will open a position, under the
[bounded-loss policy](/policies/), and file an
[opening record](/records/) you are not allowed to revise. It stays open until
week 12. Over the following ten weeks each lecture will describe one specific
way for it to die, and you will check whether that one is happening to you.

In week 12 this lecture runs again — the same slides, the same two numbers —
and by then you will be sitting in a room where roughly nine people made money
and can tell you why. That is the version of this argument that works.
