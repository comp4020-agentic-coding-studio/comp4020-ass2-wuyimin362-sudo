Read question 4 of your [opening record](/records/) — what would make you close
it — and then read your [position log](/records/) beside it.

Find two dates. **The date you closed something that was up.** And, for anything
you still hold at a loss, **the date you bought it.** Count the days between
that second date and today.

The pattern almost everyone finds is that the first number is small and the
second is large: winners sold within a fortnight, losers held since March. This
is the **disposition effect** — realising gains early and deferring losses — and
it follows from something that sounds harmless. A loss of a given size feels
bigger than a gain of the same size. If closing at a loss is what makes the loss
real to you, then not closing feels like not having lost, and the position stays
open for reasons that have nothing to do with the position.

## "I will sell when it comes back"

This is the sentence the whole week turns on, and it has arithmetic.

Your holding is down 33%. A dollar is now 67 cents. To get back to where you
started, that 67 cents has to become a dollar:

```
$1.00 / $0.67  =  1.493
```

**It has to rise 49.3%, not 33%.** The move you are waiting for is half again as
large as the move that hurt you, and nothing about the position makes that more
likely than it was before.

You have seen this arithmetic already. Week 5 showed that a fall of 50% and a
rise of 50% leave you at `0.50 × 1.50 = 0.75`, down a quarter after two moves
that sound like they cancel. It is the same asymmetry, met from the other side:
there, it explained why leverage is worse than its multiple; here, it explains
why waiting is a worse plan than it feels like.

The deeper the hole, the more this bites. Down 50% needs +100%. Down 80% needs
+400%. "When it comes back" is not a plan, it is a description of the size of
the move you now require somebody else to deliver.

## What holding it is actually costing

The loss you can see is the one in the price. The one you cannot see is in the
counterfactual column, and from week 4 you have been recording it.

Total it for the held position: what you would have if you had closed on the day
you first thought about closing, against what you have now. That difference did
not happen to you. You chose it, every morning, by not acting — which is the
uncomfortable part, because it never felt like a decision.

And notice what a held loser is in the vocabulary of week 3. Holding it is a
sizing decision you are making again each day, at today's prices, without
writing it down. If you would not open that position at today's price and at
today's percentage of your account, you are holding it for a reason that is not
about the position.

## Why this one is on the assignment

Nobody hands you this. It is available to every student in every market, it
needs no crisis and no bad luck, and the evidence for it is already written in
your own log with dates you wrote yourself. That makes it one of the six the
[final project](/assessments/final-project/) will accept, and one of the easiest
to evidence cleanly.

You are not being asked to take the disposition effect on authority. It is in
your log or it is not. If your log shows the opposite pattern — losers cut
quickly, winners held — bring it, because that is a finding too, and a rarer
one than the room expects.

## This week

Three lines in the log, today:

1. The recovery your worst position now requires, as a percentage, computed the
   way it is computed above.
2. The counterfactual total for that position since you first considered
   closing it.
3. Whether you would open it today, at today's price, at today's size. Yes or
   no, and nothing else.

The third line is the one people leave until last and then decline to write.
